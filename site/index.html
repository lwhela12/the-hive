What it is

There is already enough to go around

What stops you cold is somebody else’s Tuesday afternoon. The mountain you’ve been staring at is a molehill to the person sitting across from you — and the thing they’re stuck on is the thing you happen to do for fun.

Between us we have almost everything anyone needs. It just sits scattered across a dozen heads, and nobody knows what anybody else can do. HIVE pools it.

The other half is the asking. You talk it through until the wish is specific enough to be granted — not “I want to be healthier” but “I want someone to teach me three twenty-minute meals I can prep on a Sunday.” That version has an answer, and somebody in the room can grant it this week.

Where it came from

With thanks to Isaac Asimov

Once a month, a group of friends met for dinner. Every member brought a guest — and the whole evening was spent drawing that one person out.

Asimov spent years at a monthly dinner club in New York. In his autobiography I, Asimov he wrote about it as the Trap Door Spider Society, and he turned it into fiction as the Black Widowers — stories where a guest arrives with a problem he can’t solve, gets thoroughly questioned over dinner, and leaves with an answer none of them had on their own.

That’s the shape of this. Attention pointed at one person at a time. A problem that gets solved because it was finally described properly, out loud, to people who wanted to help.

Natalie Walstead took that idea and built on it — the same structure, brought into a faster world, with the tools to keep it going between dinners, and a table that’s open to everyone.

The members’ app

Nobody has to be in the room

Life doesn’t schedule itself around a monthly meeting. The app is how HIVE keeps working when we’re not all together — catch up on a meeting you missed, sharpen a wish whenever it occurs to you, and pick up the thread on your own time.
